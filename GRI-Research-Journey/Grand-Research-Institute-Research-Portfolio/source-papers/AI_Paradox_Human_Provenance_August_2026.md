FROM CODE TO CONSCIOUSNESS
The AI Paradox, the Identity Paradox, and
the Human Provenance Problem
An Integrated Doctoral Research Development Paper
Grand Research Institute
David Grand | August 2026
Research proposition
As artificial intelligence becomes increasingly capable of learning, predicting,
representing, and acting for human beings, the governance problem shifts from
controlling machine outputs to preserving the provenance, authority, and
contestability of human agency itself.
This report consolidates the AI Paradox, Legitimacy Paradox, recursive legitimacy, verification decay, contestability capacity, autonomous
mobility, wearables, digital human twins, synthetic identity, and agentic AI into a single research architecture. All figures are conceptual analytical
models unless expressly identified as empirical.

Executive Summary
The research program began with a practical governance tension: artificial intelligence is optimized to execute codified
rules while human systems routinely depend on context, adaptation, exception, judgment, and informal institutional
knowledge. Mobility made this tension observable. Autonomous vehicles, rideshare platforms, sensor networks,
algorithmic dispatch, and human drivers expose the difference between formal compliance and situated adaptation in
real time.
The Legitimacy Paradox extended the analysis from action to knowledge. Institutions rarely encounter reality directly.
They encounter records, classifications, provenance histories, models, expert judgments, and increasingly AI outputs.
Under conditions of information asymmetry and delegated epistemic authority, institutional validation can convert an
incomplete or false representation into accepted legitimacy. Recursive legitimacy describes the downstream
reinforcement that occurs when later actors treat prior institutional acceptance as evidence rather than independently
reexamining the source.
The present development moves the unit of analysis one step closer to the human. Generative AI, persistent personal
assistants, wearable sensors, digital human twins, autonomous agents, and future neural interfaces create the
possibility that a person's digital representation may become increasingly detailed while attribution of specific actions
becomes increasingly difficult. This paper names that tension the Identity Paradox and proposes the Human
Provenance Problem as a central governance construct.
NIST's Generative AI Profile identifies content provenance as a risk management mechanism because synthetic media
can make human generated and AI generated content difficult to distinguish. C2PA develops technical standards for
source and history of digital media and includes human and organizational identity recommendations. The proposed
contribution here is to extend provenance from content to agency: not merely where an artifact came from, but who or
what possessed authority to act, speak, decide, or delegate at each point in a digital chain.
Conceptual progression. The vertical dimension denotes analytical proximity to human agency, not an empirical scale.

1. The Integrated Research Architecture
The doctoral architecture can now be expressed as seven linked stages: Code, Compliance, Adaptation, Autonomy,
Augmentation, Agency, and Identity. The sequence is not a deterministic technological forecast. It is an analytical
progression describing how governance questions change as computational systems move from executing instructions
toward mediating human perception, judgment, and action.
Code. What instructions and objectives are embedded in the system?
Compliance. Can the system satisfy formal legal, technical, and organizational rules?
Adaptation. How does the system respond when formal rules encounter ambiguous, dynamic, or conflicting real world
conditions?
Autonomy. Which decisions can the system execute without contemporaneous human direction?
Augmentation. How does AI alter human cognition, memory, perception, and decision capacity?
Agency. Who is responsible when action emerges from a distributed human machine system?
Identity. How can institutions establish that a digital representation or action genuinely corresponds to the human
whose identity it invokes?
This architecture complements established human automation research. Parasuraman, Sheridan, and Wickens
distinguish stages and levels of automation and emphasize that automation changes rather than simply removes human
work. The GRI framework extends that insight beyond function allocation into institutional legitimacy and identity: as the
system acquires greater authority, the relevant question becomes not only which function is automated, but whose
agency is being represented.
The more accurately AI can model a human, the less humanlike appearance,
language, preference, or behavior alone can establish that the human was the
source of a digital action.

2. From the Legitimacy Paradox to Human Provenance
The Legitimacy Paradox supplies the institutional mechanism. DiMaggio and Powell's institutional theory helps explain
why organizations converge on accepted practices and validated representations: legitimacy pressures can encourage
adoption of institutionally recognized forms even where independent verification is costly. The implication for AI is not
that institutional actors abandon rationality, but that verification itself is resource intensive and can be delegated.
Recursive legitimacy occurs when one validator's acceptance becomes an input to the next validator. Verification decay
occurs when each downstream actor increasingly relies on inherited validation rather than returning to the underlying
source. Contestability capacity concerns whether the affected actor has the legal standing, technical access, resources,
time, and procedural opportunity required to challenge the accepted representation.
The antiquities case and AI are structurally analogous only at this level. They are not morally equivalent. In illicit
antiquities markets, provenance may be deliberately fabricated. In AI systems, error can arise from training data,
inference, model drift, optimization choices, interface design, or legitimate uncertainty without deceptive intent. The
common governance issue is source opacity followed by downstream institutional reliance. Remedies therefore differ
even when the information architecture is comparable.
Applied to people, this chain becomes the Human Provenance Problem. A system observes behavioral traces, infers
preferences or intentions, produces a synthetic representation, and presents that representation to another institution. If
subsequent systems treat prior validation as proof of human origin, synthetic identity can acquire recursive legitimacy.

3. The Identity Paradox
The Identity Paradox describes a governance tension produced by increasingly high fidelity digital representations of
people. A person's digital identity is already distributed across credentials, accounts, transaction histories, photographs,
location traces, communication patterns, social networks, biometrics, and platform generated profiles. Generative
models add a new capability: these traces can be synthesized into representations capable of producing new language,
images, decisions, and potentially actions that resemble the person rather than merely describing them.
Research on human digital twins has already asked whether computational counterparts of individuals can be
constructed. Deepfake scholarship adds an epistemic dimension: synthetic audiovisual representations can weaken the
evidentiary role historically assigned to recordings and can interfere with a person's ability to influence how their identity
is socially represented.
The critical distinction is between identity fidelity and identity authority. Fidelity concerns how convincingly a system
resembles or predicts an individual. Authority concerns whether the individual actually authorized the representation or
action. A system can achieve high fidelity with zero legitimate authority. Governance frameworks that conflate similarity
with authorization will become increasingly vulnerable as generative systems improve.
Participatory and inferred digital identity
A useful hypothesis is that digital identity may bifurcate into participatory and inferred forms. Participatory identity is
continuously shaped through deliberate interaction, correction, consent, credentialing, and revocation. Inferred identity
is constructed primarily from passive data, third party observations, statistical similarity, and platform inference. The
distinction does not imply that nonusers are less real or less legitimate. It identifies a possible asymmetry in how
accurately, transparently, and contestably digital systems represent different people.
This is empirically testable. Researchers could compare error rates, correction pathways, attribution confidence, and
institutional reliance across representations created with active subject participation versus representations inferred
from passive data. Participation must never become a prerequisite for personhood, access, rights, or institutional
recognition.

4. Wearables, Ambient Intelligence, and the Compression of
Distance
The hardware trajectory matters because it changes the distance between cognition and computation. Screens require
deliberate access. Phones make access persistent. Watches add continuous sensing. Smart glasses can add first
person visual context. Ambient systems can operate without repeated explicit invocation. Neural interfaces, if they
become practical at scale, would compress the boundary further.
The research question is not whether any specific product inevitably produces a synthetic self. It is how increasing
continuity, sensor density, contextual awareness, and delegated authority change the evidentiary basis of identity. A
wearable system may know what a person saw; a personal model may learn how the person writes; an agent may know
which transactions the person usually approves. None of those facts alone proves that a later action was authorized by
the person.
This distinction provides a disciplined way to incorporate emerging technologies without allowing the dissertation to
become product driven. Smart glasses, neural interfaces, AI assistants, autonomous vehicles, and future devices
become empirical instances of a general variable: human AI interface proximity. The theoretical object remains
agency and provenance, not the device.

5. Autonomous Agents and Delegated Human Agency
Agentic AI sharpens the problem because representation becomes action. Emerging 2026 research on AI identity
argues that autonomous agents can transact and delegate across organizational boundaries without continuous human
supervision, exposing gaps in existing identity and accountability infrastructure. Other work proposes cryptographic
approaches that bind agent identity, authorization requests, policy, and execution context. These are emerging preprints
rather than settled doctrine, but they identify a governance problem directly aligned with the GRI research agenda.
For the dissertation, the decisive concept should be delegation provenance: an auditable chain demonstrating who
authorized an agent, the scope of that authority, what subdelegation occurred, what context governed execution, and
whether authority remained valid at the moment of action. This converts the abstract question 'Was it AI or a human?'
into a more rigorous institutional question: 'What was the authenticated chain of authority that produced this action?'
Five states of digital action
1. Direct human action: the person contemporaneously performs the act. 2. AI assisted human action: AI informs or
drafts, but the person decides and executes. 3. Authorized delegated action: an agent acts within a defined mandate. 4.
Autonomous subdelegation: an authorized agent delegates to another system. 5. Unauthorized synthetic action: a
representation acts without valid human authority. Governance must distinguish these states even when their external
outputs are behaviorally indistinguishable.
This is where the AI Paradox and Legitimacy Paradox converge. Autonomy increases the number of actions that can
occur without contemporaneous human review. Recursive legitimacy increases the probability that downstream
institutions will accept prior machine assertions. Human provenance becomes the control layer required to prevent
apparent legitimacy from substituting for authenticated authority.

6. Governance Architecture: From Content Provenance to Agency
Provenance
NIST's Generative AI Profile identifies provenance tracking, watermarking, metadata, digital fingerprinting, and human
authentication as mechanisms that can help establish the origin and history of synthetic content. C2PA provides a
complementary standards architecture for certifying source and history of media, while emphasizing that provenance
verifies assertions and associations rather than deciding whether the underlying content is normatively good or bad.
The proposed GRI contribution is to extend this logic one level upward. Content provenance asks where an artifact
came from. Model provenance asks which model and training lineage contributed to an output. Agency provenance
asks which human, organization, or machine principal possessed legitimate authority for the action and how that
authority moved through the system.
Layer Governance question Control objective
Source integrity What is the originating evidence? Prevent corrupted inputs
Content provenance How was the artifact created or modified? Trace origin and transformations
Model provenance Which model, data, and configuration contributed? Establish technical lineage
Identity binding Which principal is represented? Prevent false attribution
Delegation provenance Who authorized what scope of action? Bound agent authority
Context verification Was authority valid at execution time? Prevent stale or displaced authority
Contestability Can the affected person challenge the record? Preserve procedural agency
Remediation Can errors and downstream copies be corrected? Reverse recursive legitimacy

7. Operationalizing the Research
The next phase should convert the conceptual architecture into falsifiable constructs. The objective is not to prove that
synthetic identity inevitably displaces human identity. It is to determine the conditions under which institutional reliance
on AI generated representations increases attribution error, reduces independent verification, or weakens a person's
practical ability to contest an incorrect representation.
The matrix converts the theoretical vocabulary into candidate observable variables and evidence sources.
Candidate hypotheses
H1: As downstream institutional reliance on prior validation increases, independent source verification decreases. H2:
Higher representational fidelity increases perceived authenticity unless counterbalanced by provenance signals. H3:
Greater delegation depth increases attribution ambiguity unless authorization is persistently bound to each action. H4:
Contestability capacity moderates the persistence of erroneous digital identity representations. H5: Systems built from
active subject participation will exhibit different error and correction profiles from systems built primarily through passive
inference. H6: Increasing human AI interface proximity increases both augmentation value and provenance risk,
creating a governance tradeoff rather than a uniformly positive or negative effect.
Methods
A mixed method design is appropriate. Phase one can use comparative case studies across autonomous mobility,
digital identity, synthetic media, and agentic workflows. Phase two can construct event level provenance datasets that
record source, validator, transformation, delegation, challenge, and remediation events. Phase three can test controlled
human judgments of authenticity and authority under different provenance disclosures. Phase four can compare
institutional governance regimes and identify which controls preserve human agency without eliminating beneficial
automation.

8. Mobility as the Empirical Laboratory
Mobility remains valuable because it externalizes otherwise abstract questions of authority. A vehicle perceives an
environment, classifies objects, predicts human behavior, selects actions, and operates under formal traffic rules while
interacting with humans who communicate through convention, hesitation, eye contact, local norms, and adaptive
behavior. The same system also generates logs that can later become institutional evidence about what occurred.
The progression from driver assistance to autonomous operation allows the research to observe the transfer of
authority. Who perceived the hazard? Who selected the maneuver? What did the model infer? What did the human
know? What was the authorized operating domain? Which record is treated as authoritative after an incident? These
questions connect compliance, adaptation, autonomy, agency, and provenance within a single empirical setting.
The broader research can then test whether mechanisms first observed in mobility generalize to financial agents, digital
assistants, wearables, identity systems, and institutional decision tools. This preserves mobility as the empirical
laboratory without allowing the dissertation's theoretical contribution to become sector specific.

9. Theoretical Contribution and Boundary Conditions
The strongest version of this research does not claim that AI is becoming conscious, that digital twins are equivalent to
people, or that technological convergence is inevitable. Its contribution is institutional: increasingly capable AI changes
the relationship among representation, authority, validation, and accountability. The research asks what governance
architecture is required when systems can produce outputs that are behaviorally consistent with a person without
proving contemporaneous human authorship or authorization.
This position also avoids technological determinism. Human institutions retain design choices. They can require
provenance, limit delegation, preserve meaningful appeals, create revocation mechanisms, maintain independent
verification, and distinguish assistance from autonomous execution. Conversely, institutional incentives can accelerate
verification decay if efficiency, scale, or prestige make inherited validation cheaper than source interrogation.
Floridi and Cowls place autonomy and explicability alongside beneficence, nonmaleficence, and justice. NIST's AI RMF
similarly treats trustworthiness as a lifecycle risk management problem. The GRI architecture contributes a narrower but
potentially measurable proposition: trustworthy AI requires not only explainable outputs and responsible models, but
durable evidence of who possessed legitimate authority at the point where machine representation became
consequential action.
Potential original constructs
Human Provenance: the traceable evidentiary relationship connecting a digital representation or action to the human
authority it purports to express. Identity Paradox: the condition in which increasing fidelity of synthetic human
representation simultaneously reduces the evidentiary value of humanlike behavior as proof of human origin.
Verification Decay: declining independent source examination as representations move through institutional chains.
Recursive Legitimacy: increasing acceptance generated by repeated downstream validation. Contestability
Capacity: the practical ability of an affected person to challenge, correct, and propagate remediation of an
institutionalized representation. Delegation Provenance: the auditable chain through which authority is granted,
constrained, exercised, subdelegated, and revoked.

10. Forward Intent: GRI and David Grand
The immediate objective should be consolidation rather than expansion. The research program now contains enough
conceptual material to support a coherent doctoral platform. The next stage should convert insight into disciplined
evidence, operational definitions, and bounded research questions.
Priority 1: Establish the canonical theory paper
Produce one peer review ready conceptual paper that formally integrates the AI Paradox, Legitimacy Paradox, Identity
Paradox, and Human Provenance Problem. Define each construct, specify boundary conditions, identify competing
explanations, and state falsifiable propositions. Treat From Code to Consciousness as the intellectual origin of the
program while allowing the doctoral work to sharpen or reject earlier assumptions.
Priority 2: Build the provenance evidence architecture
Continue the object level antiquities dataset as the historical institutional case, but build a parallel event schema suitable
for AI: source, representation, validator, decision, delegation, challenge, correction, and downstream propagation. The
shared schema is what permits structural comparison without implying moral equivalence.
Priority 3: Preserve mobility as the living laboratory
Use autonomous mobility to observe compliance, contextual adaptation, transfer of control, machine perception, human
intervention, and postevent evidentiary records. This gives the broader theory an empirical anchor while maintaining
relevance to governance, sustainability, infrastructure, and global management.
Priority 4: Create a Human Provenance research stream at GRI
Organize future work under four questions: identity binding, delegation authority, contestability, and remediation.
Evaluate wearables, synthetic media, personal AI, agentic systems, and neural interfaces through these variables rather
than as isolated technologies. This prevents novelty from driving the research agenda.
Priority 5: Subject the theory to faculty level challenge
Present the work as propositions to be tested rather than conclusions to be defended. Invite critique from political
economy, global strategy, finance, leadership, institutional theory, law, ethics, and technology governance. The
objective is to identify where the framework fails, which variables are redundant, and which causal mechanisms survive
cross disciplinary scrutiny.
Forward intent
GRI's forward intent should be to develop a rigorous research program on the
governance of human agency in AI mediated systems. The program will examine
how authority moves from humans to intelligent systems, how institutional
validation can amplify representations, how individuals retain meaningful
capacity to contest machine mediated identities, and what provenance
infrastructure is required to preserve accountable human agency as AI becomes
more ambient, autonomous, and personalized.
For David Grand's doctoral trajectory, the strategic objective is to move from documenting technological change to
explaining a governance mechanism that can be observed across sectors. The strongest dissertation will not attempt to
predict every future device. It will identify the institutional conditions under which AI augmentation strengthens human
