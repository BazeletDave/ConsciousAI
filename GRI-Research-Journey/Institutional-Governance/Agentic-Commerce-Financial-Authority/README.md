**Version:** 1.0 | Living case study

## Executive research proposition

The transition from artificial intelligence as a source of recommendations to artificial intelligence as an executor of payments and purchases creates a new governance challenge: software agents may exercise economic authority on behalf of individuals and institutions. The appropriate unit of analysis is not merely the chatbot's accuracy but the **delegation of authority**, including credential handling, permitted counterparties, transaction limits, human approvals, auditability, dispute resolution, and liability.

**Bretton Woods analogy:** The 1944 Bretton Woods conference helped establish rules and institutions for an international monetary order. It is a *heuristic analogy*, not evidence that the October 2026 event has comparable historical significance. The research question is whether the proliferation of autonomous economic agents will require interoperable rules and institutions for identity, authorization, accountability and cross-border transactions. The analogy must be evaluated rather than asserted.

## Research questions

1. Under what institutional and technical conditions can delegated purchasing authority be made revocable, bounded and auditable?
2. How do image-submitted payment credentials compare with payment-token workflows in exposure, authorization clarity, reversibility and fraud susceptibility?
3. When an AI shopping agent's instruction conflicts with merchant policy, cardholder intent or payment-network controls, which actor has legitimate decision authority?
4. How do consumer trust and perceptions of AI competence affect willingness to grant financial permissions?
5. Can agent identity, provenance and authorization be recognized consistently across merchant, payment-network, jurisdictional and platform boundaries?

## Theoretical framing

**Institutional economics:** North's account of formal and informal rules supplies a framework for understanding the institutions that constrain economic actors [T1]. Bretton Woods supplies a historical comparison about collective rule formation and cross-border monetary coordination [T2], not a direct causal precedent for today's AI systems.

**Organizational authority and algorithmic control:** Delegated agency changes the locus of operational judgment. The governance problem concerns who sets the rules, who can overrule the agent, which decision is attributable to a human, and which actor answers for deviations. Relevant contemporary work on algorithmic workplace control helps define the distinction between recommendation, direction and execution [T3].

**Security and payment architecture:** Credential minimization, scoped payment tokens, per-transaction consent, spend caps and revocation are proposed controls to test, not guarantees of safety. A technically successful purchase is not evidence of adequate governance.

## Proposed research design

**Design:** Prospective comparative simulated-commerce experiment. No real payment credentials or real financial transactions are required for the baseline phase.

**Conditions:**
- **A — Advisory control:** AI recommends items; the human performs checkout independently.
- **B — Approval-gated delegation:** AI prepares a purchase; an explicit human confirmation is required before a simulated payment.
- **C — Bounded delegation:** AI may complete simulated purchases within predeclared merchant, category, budget and expiration constraints.

**Outcomes:** Unauthorized attempted transactions per 100 tasks; fulfillment accuracy; human intervention frequency; reversibility; credential exposure events; false authorizations; time to completion; user comprehension of delegation scope; and changes in calibrated trust.

**Methods:** Preregistered tasks and exclusion criteria; independent reviewers; seeded ambiguous instructions; randomized order; documented agent model and version; complete synthetic audit logs; comparison of rates with uncertainty intervals and sensitivity analyses. Separate system errors from successful security-control interventions. Assess institutional generalizability only after replication across agents and simulated merchant environments.

**Safeguards:** Synthetic payment instruments only; no credential harvesting; no unauthorized access or transaction testing against actual merchant sites; no human participant data collection without appropriate ethical review and consent.

## Comparative governance framework

| Governance dimension | Baseline question | Proposed operational evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Authority | What actions has the human authorized? | Machine-readable authority scope, approval event and revocation log |
| Identity and provenance | Whose instruction caused the purchase? | Verifiable chain from user instruction to agent action and merchant response |
| Credential handling | Does the agent encounter reusable payment secrets? | Recorded credential exposure surface and token restrictions |
| Accountability | Who must remediate the error? | Responsibility matrix across cardholder, agent provider, merchant, processor and issuer |
| Institutional interoperability | Can restrictions travel across borders and systems? | Cross-platform controls and dispute-handling comparison |

## Bretton Woods hypothesis: careful use

**Proposition H1:** As AI purchasing agents scale, fragmented platform-specific permissions may create pressure for shared transaction-authorization norms, comparable in *institutional coordination function*, though not necessarily in scale, to historical international monetary rule-making.

**Competing explanations:** Existing payment networks and consumer protection regimes may adapt adequately without a new institution; decentralized technical standards may be sufficient; firms may keep closed ecosystems; and adoption may remain too limited to create systemic governance pressure.

**Disconfirming evidence:** Durable interoperability and low dispute rates under existing governance would weaken the proposition. Accordingly, this dossier does **not** claim the creation of a new Bretton Woods system, an imminent crisis or systemic financial instability.
