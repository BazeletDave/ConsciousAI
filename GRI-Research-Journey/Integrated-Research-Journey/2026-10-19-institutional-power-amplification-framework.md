# GRI Institutional Power Amplification Framework

**Grand Research Institute | David A. Grand | 19 October 2026**

## Research thesis

AI may amplify institutional capabilities while changing who can access them, who exercises authority, and who experiences their consequences. This is an original GRI research proposition, not a validated causal finding. Historical political economy and management cases provide comparisons, not evidence that their actors used contemporary AI.

## Five analytical dimensions

1. **Access:** cost, permissions, literacy, connectivity, proprietary data, compute and institutional gatekeeping.
2. **Capability:** analysis, prediction, production, coordination and autonomous execution.
3. **Authority:** legitimate decision rights, delegation, governance, review and remedies.
4. **Consequences:** economic value, distribution, public welfare, environmental effects and institutional dependence.
5. **Feedback:** changed behavior and data alter the next decisions, institutions and rules.

## Comparative research agenda

- IMF intervention in South Korea: stabilization, sovereignty, creditor influence and distributional consequences. Ask whether AI-supported intervention changes decision speed, legitimacy or recourse.
- IFC development finance and WTO disputes: financing, endorsement, formal rights, bargaining leverage and actual remedies. Ask how algorithmic coordination changes these relationships.
- Washington Consensus, Packenham, Dell Brazil and Intel Costa Rica: policy durability, institutions, subnational bargaining and implementation capacity. Test whether technical readiness diverges from political readiness.
- Start-Up Chile and education access: public support, access to knowledge, entrepreneurship and continuity across administrations. Investigate whether AI broadens opportunity or concentrates scarce resources elsewhere.
- Historical public research funding, specialized software, publishing and online learning: trace how access to costly tools and institutional knowledge changed over time. Funding examples and individuals require source-level verification before inclusion as factual findings.
- Contemporary frontier AI firms and infrastructure: compare capital investment, compute, energy, data, model distribution, deployment authority and measurable outcomes. Distinguish personal wealth from corporate budgets and plans from realized capacity.
- GRI 1MDB and Cambodian antiquities comparisons: examine different mechanisms of signal escalation, provenance validation, legitimacy and institutional correction. The 1MDB competition draft remains unpublished.

## Hypotheses for testing

**H1 (access redistribution):** Lower tool costs expand participation, conditional on digital skills, connectivity and access to relevant data. Rival: gains accrue chiefly to incumbent institutions.

**H2 (power amplification):** Greater capability increases the reach or speed of decisions, conditional on authorization and deployment infrastructure. Rival: organizational bottlenecks prevent meaningful effects.

**H3 (governance gap):** Capability and deployment changes can outpace institutional review and remedy. Rival: AI strengthens oversight enough to narrow the gap.

**H4 (feedback):** AI-mediated decisions can change the behaviors and data used for later decisions. Rival: observed shifts are driven by external market or policy changes.

**H5 (institutional variation):** Identical AI tools yield different outcomes across political, financial and organizational settings. Rival: technical implementation and product-market fit explain the differences.

## November 2026 crosswalk | Institutional infrastructure and algorithmic management

This bounded synthesis applies the existing five dimensions to two already documented GRI cases: [Texas AI infrastructure institutional decisions](2026-11-03-texas-ai-infrastructure-institutional-event.md) and [Uber and Lyft longitudinal field research](../Algorithmic-Management/Uber-Lyft-Research/README.md). It is an analytical comparison, not a claim that these cases share a common causal mechanism.

| Existing dimension | Texas AI infrastructure | Platform algorithmic management | Governance question |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Access | Interconnection queue and environmental permission | Driver access to offers, dispatch and platform information | Who controls entry and visibility, and under what rules? |
| Capability | Grid planning and project analytics | Dynamic dispatch, pricing and matching | What can the system optimize, and what remains unobserved? |
| Authority | PUCT, ERCOT, TCEQ, executives and investors have distinct responsibilities | Platform operators, drivers and regulators hold different decision rights | Who can approve, contest, correct or reverse consequential decisions? |
| Consequences | Project sequencing, infrastructure costs and investment exposure require project evidence | Gross offers, realized earnings, expenses and worker exposure require transaction evidence | Who bears costs, and what outcome is actually measured? |
| Feedback | Revised queue requests, permits and investment plans | Acceptance, cancellation, incentives and dispatch feedback | Do system responses change incentives or accountability over time? |

**Comparative proposition.** Faster machine optimization may increase the volume of consequential decisions faster than institutions can validate causal explanations and provide effective recourse. This is a hypothesis consistent with the AI Paradox, not an independently established general law.

**Evidence constraints.** ERCOT connection requests are not constructed capacity. Announced investment is not incurred expenditure. A displayed rideshare offer is not realized net income. Observed changes in platform offers do not reveal proprietary dispatch intent. Regulatory timing and market valuation movements do not establish causation. Each case requires its own unit of analysis, date, denominator, counterfactual and alternative explanations.

**Public research boundary.** This crosswalk contains only public-facing conceptual synthesis and references to existing research. It does not describe confidential GRI review arrangements, institutional relationships, unpublished observations or proprietary operational methods.
