# GRI Institutional Power Amplification Framework

**Grand Research Institute | David A. Grand | 19 October 2026**

## Research thesis

AI may amplify institutional capabilities while changing who can access them, who exercises authority, and who experiences their consequences. This is an original GRI research proposition, not a validated causal finding. Historical political economy and management cases provide comparisons, not evidence that their actors used contemporary AI.

## Five analytical dimensions

1. **Access:** cost, permissions, literacy, connectivity, proprietary data, compute and institutional gatekeeping.
2. **Capability:** analysis, prediction, production, coordination and autonomous execution.
3. **Authority:** legitimate decision rights, delegation, governance, review and remedies.
4. **Consequences:** economic value, distribution, public welfare, environmental effects and institutional dependence.
5. **Feedback:** changed behavior and data alter the next decisions, institutions and rules.

## Comparative research agenda

- IMF intervention in South Korea: stabilization, sovereignty, creditor influence and distributional consequences. Ask whether AI-supported intervention changes decision speed, legitimacy or recourse.
- IFC development finance and WTO disputes: financing, endorsement, formal rights, bargaining leverage and actual remedies. Ask how algorithmic coordination changes these relationships.
- Washington Consensus, Packenham, Dell Brazil and Intel Costa Rica: policy durability, institutions, subnational bargaining and implementation capacity. Test whether technical readiness diverges from political readiness.
- Start-Up Chile and education access: public support, access to knowledge, entrepreneurship and continuity across administrations. Investigate whether AI broadens opportunity or concentrates scarce resources elsewhere.
- Historical public research funding, specialized software, publishing and online learning: trace how access to costly tools and institutional knowledge changed over time. Funding examples and individuals require source-level verification before inclusion as factual findings.
- Contemporary frontier AI firms and infrastructure: compare capital investment, compute, energy, data, model distribution, deployment authority and measurable outcomes. Distinguish personal wealth from corporate budgets and plans from realized capacity.
- GRI 1MDB and Cambodian antiquities comparisons: examine different mechanisms of signal escalation, provenance validation, legitimacy and institutional correction. The 1MDB competition draft remains unpublished.

## Hypotheses for testing

**H1 (access redistribution):** Lower tool costs expand participation, conditional on digital skills, connectivity and access to relevant data. Rival: gains accrue chiefly to incumbent institutions.

**H2 (power amplification):** Greater capability increases the reach or speed of decisions, conditional on authorization and deployment infrastructure. Rival: organizational bottlenecks prevent meaningful effects.

**H3 (governance gap):** Capability and deployment changes can outpace institutional review and remedy. Rival: AI strengthens oversight enough to narrow the gap.

**H4 (feedback):** AI-mediated decisions can change the behaviors and data used for later decisions. Rival: observed shifts are driven by external market or policy changes.

**H5 (institutional variation):** Identical AI tools yield different outcomes across political, financial and organizational settings. Rival: technical implementation and product-market fit explain the differences.

## November 2026 crosswalk | Institutional infrastructure and algorithmic management

This bounded synthesis applies the existing five dimensions to two already documented GRI cases: [Texas AI infrastructure institutional decisions](2026-11-03-texas-ai-infrastructure-institutional-event.md) and [Uber and Lyft longitudinal field research](../Algorithmic-Management/Uber-Lyft-Research/README.md). It is an analytical comparison, not a claim that these cases share a common causal mechanism.

| Existing dimension | Texas AI infrastructure | Platform algorithmic management | Governance question |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Access | Interconnection queue and environmental permission | Driver access to offers, dispatch and platform information | Who controls entry and visibility, and under what rules? |
| Capability | Grid planning and project analytics | Dynamic dispatch, pricing and matching | What can the system optimize, and what remains unobserved? |
| Authority | PUCT, ERCOT, TCEQ, executives and investors have distinct responsibilities | Platform operators, drivers and regulators hold different decision rights | Who can approve, contest, correct or reverse consequential decisions? |
| Consequences | Project sequencing, infrastructure costs and investment exposure require project evidence | Gross offers, realized earnings, expenses and worker exposure require transaction evidence | Who bears costs, and what outcome is actually measured? |
| Feedback | Revised queue requests, permits and investment plans | Acceptance, cancellation, incentives and dispatch feedback | Do system responses change incentives or accountability over time? |

**Comparative proposition.** Faster machine optimization may increase the volume of consequential decisions faster than institutions can validate causal explanations and provide effective recourse. This is a hypothesis consistent with the AI Paradox, not an independently established general law.

**Evidence constraints.** ERCOT connection requests are not constructed capacity. Announced investment is not incurred expenditure. A displayed rideshare offer is not realized net income. Observed changes in platform offers do not reveal proprietary dispatch intent. Regulatory timing and market valuation movements do not establish causation. Each case requires its own unit of analysis, date, denominator, counterfactual and alternative explanations.

**Public research boundary.** This crosswalk contains only public-facing conceptual synthesis and references to existing research. It does not describe confidential GRI review arrangements, institutional relationships, unpublished observations or proprietary operational methods.

**Cross-case consistency rules.** Preserve the unit, time window, population, denominator and provenance of every numerical claim. Never compare interconnection request megawatts with operating capacity, displayed gross earnings with realized net income, or stock capitalization with project cash losses. Do not treat absent access to a proprietary algorithm as evidence of its intent. Human authority is assessed through observable visibility, discretion, contestability, correction and accountability, not through titles alone.

**Current synthesis:** The cases support a comparative institutional research question, but they do not yet establish a shared quantitative causal effect or independently verified general law. See the linked case documents for source-specific qualifications. No confidential institutional operations or proprietary research procedures are included.

## Frontier laboratory research access and institutional authority | November 2026

**Research question.** Does wider participation in frontier AI research entail corresponding decision rights over research priorities, compute allocation, deployment, challenge and correction? This is a bounded comparative test of the existing access, capability, authority, consequences and feedback framework, not a new research program or a finding about undisclosed internal arrangements.

**Publicly documented comparison (reviewed November 6, 2026).** The units below are distinct published pathways, not equivalent positions or representative samples of all laboratory researchers. “Not established” means the reviewed public program description does not establish a particular right, not that the right is absent.

| Institution and pathway | Documented access and capability | Research agenda influence | Deployment, challenge or correction authority | Evidence disposition |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Anthropic Research Residency (2026) | Six week to six month paid placements, mentorship, frontier model access and dedicated project compute; technical leadership backgrounds welcomed without prior ML experience | Residents work on problems outlined by Anthropic researchers and matched to interests and backgrounds | Independent deployment veto, compute governance or formal challenge authority **not established** by the residency announcement | Primary program description; evidence of research participation, not transferred governance rights |
| OpenAI Residency (2026) | Six month full time research employment, mentorship and hands on frontier research | Residents and mentors collaboratively define and scope projects aligned with OpenAI's mission | Independent deployment or corrective authority **not established** by the residency description | Primary program description; 2026 applications listed as closed |
| OpenAI Safety Fellowship (2026) | External safety research, mentorship, stipend, compute support and potential remote participation; explicitly no internal system access | Fellows pursue empirically grounded research within stated priority areas | Internal deployment or system intervention authority **not established**; the program explicitly excludes internal system access | Primary fellowship description; analytically distinct from the employee residency |
| Google DeepMind / Google Student Researcher Program (2026–2027) | Paid, in person 12–24 week research placements for enrolled degree students with host teams across Google AI | Collaboration on host research projects; independent agenda setting **not established** | Independent deployment, challenge or corrective authority **not established** by program description | Primary program description; enrollment and location requirements limit comparability |
| Microsoft Research Cambridge Residency | Two year multidisciplinary research team position, mentorship, network access and research collaboration | Contribution to team projects; autonomous priority setting **not established** | Independent deployment, challenge or corrective authority **not established** by program description | Primary program description; different duration and organizational role |
| Microsoft Research / CMU AI agent evaluation workshop (October 2026) | Cross sector convening to develop evaluation measurement approaches and public synthesis | Participants can contribute critique and standards discussions | Binding deployment decision authority **not established** by the event description | Public convening evidence, **not** an employment or residency pathway |

**Primary sources.** [Anthropic Research Residency](https://alignment.anthropic.com/2026/anthropic-residency/); [OpenAI Residency](https://openai.com/residency/); [OpenAI Safety Fellowship](https://alignment.openai.com/safety-fellowship); [Google Student Researcher Program](https://deepmind.google/student-researcher-program/); [Microsoft Cambridge Residency](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/academic-program/cambridge-residency/); [Microsoft Research / CMU agent evaluation workshop](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/event/advancing-the-science-of-ai-agent-evaluation/).

**Initial observation.** All six reviewed descriptions publicly identify pathways for research participation, collaboration or critique. None of these *program descriptions* establishes an independent participant right to authorize, stop or reverse consequential model deployments. This is a **bounded documentary observation**, not proof that frontier laboratories lack oversight, that research participants never influence governance, or that concentration of authority causes safety failures. The OpenAI Safety Fellowship provides a particularly explicit distinction between external research support and internal system access.

**Falsifiable propositions and next evidence thresholds.** H1: documented research access is more common than documented independent deployment authority in comparable public program descriptions. Disconfirm through a published program charter conferring specified independent deployment rights. H2: external research pathways more often constrain operational system access than internal research positions; test with comparable written access rules rather than inference from program labels. H3: research participation may influence institutional practice indirectly even without formal veto rights; test only against dated documented adoption, escalation, policy changes or decision records. Future comparisons must distinguish formal decision rights from advisory influence and avoid equating nondisclosure with absence. No internal access, institutional affiliation, causal effect or independent empirical reproduction is claimed.
