### Research thesis
The global AI divide is not solely an adoption or digital literacy gap. It may also reflect decades of unequal access to affordable reliable energy, development finance, grid resilience, computing infrastructure, investment capital, and institutional decision-making authority. GRI asks how historical infrastructure finance and governance shape AI opportunity, technological dependency, local value capture, and resilience.

### GRI analytical pathways
1. Historic financing, energy policy and investment → electricity cost, reliability and grid capacity.
2. Electricity and capital constraints → feasibility and location of domestic compute, data centers and digital infrastructure.
3. Ownership, regulation, institutions and labor capabilities → AI adoption and distribution of economic value.
4. Climate shocks, grid redundancy and recovery capacity → resilience of AI services and communities.
5. External compute and finance dependence → potential sovereignty and accountability tradeoffs.

**Competing outcomes:** infrastructure can enable local AI opportunity, or concentrate returns with external owners while raising local energy/water pressures. Neither outcome should be presumed without evidence. Imported cloud services and smaller efficient models may reduce domestic infrastructure requirements.

### Research questions
- Does historical energy investment predict subsequent AI readiness after controls for income, education, institutions and connectivity?
- When do development finance and public-private arrangements retain benefits locally?
- How do grid resilience and climate exposure modify the relationship between compute access and operational AI capability?
- Can shared compute, regional coordination and distributed energy help countries reduce AI dependency?
- Which of Nelson's institutional perspectives best explains different country outcomes, and under what conditions?

### Comparative political economy | Public research, national development and the AI Paradox (1 November 2026)

**Research proposition, not a historical verdict:** Public investment in fundamental research, infrastructure and human capital can enable private innovation and national technological capability. Commercialization arrangements, institutional capacity, international integration and the distribution of property and decision rights affect who benefits. Government funding is neither a sufficient nor necessary explanation of any country's development trajectory; competing factors include geography, migration, institutions, education, private capital, external constraints and historical inequality.

**Comparative framework:**
| Setting | Institutional mechanisms to examine | Rival explanations and cautions |
| --- | --- | --- |
| United States | Federal research and procurement, universities, venture finance, migration, commercialization, intellectual property and private platforms | Avoid attributing prosperity to one cause or assuming government knows all technology before launch |
| China | Industrial policy, manufacturing ecosystems, infrastructure, public and private technology investment, scale and global trade | Distinguish sectors and time periods; evaluate capital allocation, demographics, governance and external demand |
| India | Digital public infrastructure, software services, telecommunications, entrepreneurial activity, public investment and inclusion | Examine unequal connectivity, regional disparities, regulation and differences between adoption and domestic value capture |
| Cuba | Education and scientific capacity, state institutions, capital and technology access, trade restrictions and external shocks | Distinguish the effects of domestic policy, US sanctions, market size and historical conditions; do not infer a single causal explanation |
| Metropolitan Park, Queens | Local urban infrastructure, public approvals, private development, transport integration, accessibility, digital services and public benefits | Urban district is a different unit of analysis from sovereign states; use only carefully specified transferable mechanisms |

**Academic crosswalk:** Landry Signé's published development and AI-divide research motivates tests of technological leapfrogging, access to infrastructure and value distribution. Roy C. Nelson's institutional political economy lenses motivate analysis of markets and cooperation, state strategy, and structural asymmetries. GRI's own AI Paradox asks whether successful technological optimization can produce second-order harms, dependency or legitimacy gaps. These are GRI interpretations, not faculty endorsements.

**Public-to-private value chain to test:** public research and infrastructure → knowledge diffusion and workforce capabilities → entrepreneurial or institutional adoption → ownership and market structure → measurable productivity and public outcomes → feedback, distributional effects and governance adaptation. At each stage, measure alternative explanations and who captures benefits and bears costs.

**Hypotheses:** (H1) Public research spillovers are more likely to generate broad gains where complementary infrastructure, human capital and competitive access exist. (H2) Rapid leapfrogging without robust institutions can increase dependency and unequal benefits despite high adoption. (H3) Stronger independent assurance, contestability and recovery mechanisms can reduce second-order harms from AI-driven optimization. These are proposed tests, not established causal findings.

**Research controls:** Compare like units and defined periods; separate public grants, procurement, tax incentives and private financing; distinguish basic science from downstream products; separate publication obligations from ownership and licensing rights; use primary funding and patent records, peer-reviewed economic history, World Bank/IMF indicators and documented counterexamples. ### Comparative research design | National innovation systems, leapfrogging and urban AI governance (1 November 2026)

**Purpose:** Strengthen methodological rigor within the existing GRI Integrated Research Journey. This is a public conceptual and evidence protocol, not a new study, research grant, client engagement, deployed model or publication of confidential GRI intellectual property.

#### Unit of analysis and causal identification

Separate **country-year** comparisons (United States, China, India, Cuba), **policy or investment episodes** (research grants, public procurement, digital infrastructure, industrial policy), and **urban project-stage** observations (Metropolitan Park, Queens). These units must not be pooled into a single regression or used to claim that a national development mechanism automatically applies to one urban district.

The proposed causal chain is: institutional rules and financing → scientific and infrastructural capability → technology diffusion and commercialization → productivity, access and value capture → distributional and systemic consequences → institutional feedback. Each arrow requires its own evidence and possible counterexamples. Do not infer causation from temporal sequence alone.

#### Proposition and falsification register

| Proposition | Observable mechanism | Candidate indicators | Rival explanation or falsifier |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| P1: Complementary public investment can increase private innovation | Public R&D, procurement and infrastructure reduce early-stage risk and build absorptive capacity | Public R&D by instrument, patents with government-supported citations, private follow-on investment, productivity | Private capital, education, immigration or market size explain outcomes better; effects vanish under credible controls |
| P2: Leapfrogging can widen access without equalizing value capture | Adoption accelerates through shared infrastructure while ownership and data rents remain concentrated | Adoption by income and region, local supplier participation, domestic capabilities, digital service reliability | Benefits are broadly distributed or intermediate infrastructure remains indispensable |
| P3: Institutional capacity moderates AI's social outcomes | Appeals, independent validation and operational resilience alter the costs of automation failure | Error and appeal rates, remediation latency, recovery time, service exclusion | Similar outcomes emerge without these controls or controls are ineffective |
| P4: Venue optimization may displace externalities | Faster entry and optimized transport change crowd, curb and neighborhood demand | Entry-time distribution, pedestrian access, transit reliability, curb congestion, accessibility | Area-wide effects improve, or exogenous attendance patterns explain changes |
| P5: Public-private innovation can produce an AI Paradox | Technical efficiency rises while accountability, privacy or systemic resilience deteriorates | Service efficiency jointly with breach, exclusion, concentration and override measures | Both efficiency and social outcomes improve; adverse outcomes cannot be attributed to the intervention |

#### Evidence hierarchy and analytic discipline

1. **Primary administrative evidence:** government budget and grant records, official planning and environmental records, procurement notices, statutory or regulatory decisions and audited disclosures. A filing establishes what was reported or approved, not necessarily delivered.
2. **Peer-reviewed and scholarly evidence:** use research design, sample, causal identification, limitations and competing findings, not merely an abstract or citation count.
3. **Industry and project claims:** retain publisher, date, financial incentives, project phase and independent corroboration status.
4. **GRI field observations:** retain collection method, authorization, provenance and limitations; do not extrapolate anecdotes to population effects.
5. **Scenarios and synthetic tests:** explicitly label as hypothetical; simulation metrics cannot be promoted to production performance.

For every material claim preserve: claim ID; jurisdiction and unit; period; source title, publisher, URL and access date; document version; original excerpt or page reference; evidence classification; exact measured variable and denominator; counterevidence; confidence rationale; rights and confidentiality status; reviewer and revision history. Public notes may describe this standard without publishing proprietary GRI evaluation instruments.

#### Methods and comparative validity

For country comparisons, consider longitudinal descriptive analysis before causal designs. Where data permit, test fixed effects, lagged covariates, event studies or synthetic controls with documented assumptions and sensitivity analyses. Account for reverse causality (wealth enabling innovation investment), omitted institutional factors, cross-border spillovers, selection into funding and differences in data quality. For Metropolitan Park, begin with a dated document chronology and policy process tracing; before-after comparisons require a real operational baseline and appropriate comparison locations. Never represent an announced technology as a deployed treatment.

### GRI independent quantitative replication | India UPI and Findex (1 November 2026)

**Executed calculation, not merely a proposed study.** Using six official NPCI monthly UPI transaction volume observations (millions): April 2026 22,346.80; May 23,201.93; June 22,716.07; July 23,658.35; August 24,508.96; September 24,068.38. Source: [NPCI UPI product statistics](https://www.npci.org.in/product/upi/product-statistics), accessed 1 November 2026. This is an independently computed descriptive replication of aggregate figures, not replication of the IMF's confidential or granular transaction-level causal analysis.

**Exact reproducible calculations:** six-month sum 140,500.49 million (140.50049 billion); September versus April +7.7039%. Ordinary least squares of natural log monthly volume on a sequential month index 0–5 yields slope 0.01646093 log points per month, equivalent to 1.6597% compound monthly trend, R-squared 0.77349 and conventional two-sided slope p=0.02091 (n=6). Conventional 95% Student-t slope confidence interval transformed to compound percent units: approximately +0.4103% to +2.9246% monthly. The small, serially ordered sample makes conventional OLS significance fragile; no seasonality, serial correlation or structural breaks were modeled. Do not extrapolate this six-month trend as an annual growth forecast.

**Cross-sectional distribution check:** [World Bank India digital finance country snapshot](https://digitalfinance.worldbank.org/country/india) reports Global Findex 2024 digital-payment participation rates: women 36%, men 54%, a gap of 18 percentage points; poorest 40% 43%, wealthiest 60% 56%, a gap of 13 percentage points. These are independently calculated differences from reported rounded survey rates, not newly estimated population parameters. No survey standard errors, microdata weighting or statistical significance tests were reproduced. Different years and populations mean these disparities must not be attributed to 2026 UPI transaction growth or to UPI specifically.

**Long-horizon descriptive check:** [India Ministry of Finance 24 August 2026 release](https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleseDetailm.aspx?PRID=2302657&lang=1&reg=3) reports 1.78 crore UPI transactions in FY2016–17 and 24,162 crore in FY2025–26. Endpoint ratio is approximately 13,574 times and nine-interval compound annual growth approximately 187.87%, with extreme sensitivity to the tiny initial base and to fiscal-year definitions. This is not a causal estimate.

**Reproduction specification (Python, numpy/scipy):**
```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import linregress, t
v=np.array([22346.80,23201.93,22716.07,23658.35,24508.96,24068.38])
fit=linregress(np.arange(6),np.log(v))
print(v.sum()/1000,100*(v[-1]/v[0]-1),100*np.expm1(fit.slope),fit.rvalue**2)
ci=t.ppf(.975,4)*fit.stderr
print(100*np.expm1(fit.slope-ci),100*np.expm1(fit.slope+ci))
print('Findex 2024 percentage point gaps:',54-36,56-43)
```

**Inference:** P2 is supported descriptively as rapid payment-system scaling with continuing inclusion gaps; these outcomes coexist but cannot be causally linked from aggregate data. P1, P3, P4 and P5 remain untested by this regression. The IMF's [2025 interoperability study](https://www.imf.org/en/publications/fintech-notes/issues/2025/06/25/growing-retail-digital-payments-the-value-of-interoperability-567814) supplies an independently published causal argument, but GRI has not replicated its underlying transaction-level identification or data. A credible next causal step requires accessible microdata or a justified comparison group, pretrends, controls, sensitivity analysis and independent reproduction. Preserve confidentiality and do not characterize these calculations as proof that UPI or AI caused inclusion disparities.
