## GRI original analytical extension
**The AI policy observation paradox:** monetary and development institutions may need to govern rapidly evolving AI-driven capital formation with lagged measurements, uncertain forward scenarios and uneven international exposure. Investment may simultaneously create short-term demand, electricity and financing pressures and longer-run productivity capacity. Policy impacts cross borders, and unequal access to energy, compute, finance and resilient institutions can widen the global AI divide even while aggregate investment rises.

**Nelson institutional political economy (GRI application):** liberalism frames international investment and coordination; economic nationalism frames domestic industrial, energy and technology priorities; structuralism frames capital concentration, debt constraints and asymmetric ownership. These perspectives are applied independently by GRI, not attributed as new statements by Professor Roy C. Nelson.

**Landry Signé connection:** Signé's public scholarship on the global AI divide foregrounds unequal compute, electricity, supply chains, institutional participation and strategies for inclusive development. This event supplies a contemporary macroeconomic policy counterpart; it does not prove any causal claim in Signé's work.

**World Bank / IMF / resilience:** test how historical development finance and electricity reliability affect the ability of countries to capture AI investment benefits, with debt sustainability, price stability, grid resilience and climate exposure as moderators. ## Empirical study design
Research question: Can investment, grid load, compute capacity and capital allocation data provide useful leading indicators of AI-driven structural transformation beyond lagged GDP, CPI and employment reports?

Candidate observables: announced vs realized data-center capex, equipment orders, electricity interconnection queues, grid capacity, industrial electricity tariffs, semiconductor imports, labor productivity, wage distributions, CPI/PPI, credit conditions, sovereign spreads, IMF AIPI, World Bank project financing and IFC/MIGA disclosures. ## Transcript continuation: AI demand, supply, monetary policy and institutional uncertainty
**Source status:** The following is GRI's original analytical paraphrase of a second partial transcript passage supplied by the author. Exact spoken words, speaker labels, timing and completeness require verification against an official recording or transcript before quotation.

Georgieva's question connects energy shocks, war-related disruptions, and uneven global investment enthusiasm about AI. She asks how monetary policy should evaluate AI's short- and long-run effects and what policymakers may be underweighting.

The response identifies three distinct channels:
1. **Near-term demand before supply:** AI-related capital spending can increase aggregate demand before productivity benefits emerge. The speaker describes a prospective positive supply shock over the medium term, while warning that central banks must evaluate its inflation and employment consequences along the transition.
2. **Central-bank analytical transformation:** AI tools may help policy institutions process extensive, noisy, lagged and sometimes mismeasured economic data. This is a forecast about analytical capability, not evidence that models eliminate measurement error, hallucination, data bias or judgment.
3. **Real-economy and market-structure uncertainty:** Productivity and aggregate wealth may increase, but the employment transition, distribution of profits, business failures and concentration of market power are uncertain. Investment outcomes depend on who can capture returns and how markets are structured.

The speaker also describes an external productivity-and-jobs task force and a proposed Bay Area field hearing on November 16. **This scheduling claim comes from the supplied passage and has not been independently verified here.**

### GRI research extension: the temporal AI investment paradox
AI infrastructure expansion can raise near-term demand for electricity, semiconductors, construction, financing and skilled labor even when the anticipated medium-run supply effect is disinflationary through productivity. These effects differ across countries and may be amplified by energy constraints, climate exposure and import dependence. The timing and net effect are empirical questions, not established forecasts.

### GRI research extension: the central-bank epistemic paradox
AI could help central banks extract signals from large datasets while also introducing model dependence, correlated errors, opaque feature selection and new governance risks. Improved forecasting accuracy must be demonstrated with out-of-sample benchmarks, historical vintages and human review rather than inferred from access to more information.

### Proposed evidence and evaluation
- Compare announced and realized AI capex, grid interconnection and power prices, semiconductor shipments, credit conditions, labor demand and productivity, preserving vintage and publication lags.
- Separate short-run demand effects from medium-run supply effects with alternative identification strategies, energy-shock controls and country heterogeneity.
- Test whether AI-assisted nowcasts outperform transparent conventional baselines on vintage-correct holdout data, with error calibration, robustness and human accountability.
- Evaluate distribution of gains by labor, firm size, location and domestic versus foreign ownership; distinguish market concentration from proven anticompetitive conduct.
- Apply Nelson's institutional perspectives and Signé's global AI divide research as analytical lenses; use IMF macroeconomic indicators, World Bank energy-finance projects and resilience indicators to examine cross-border differences.

## Transcript continuation: NHI, financial stability and independent task forces
**Evidence status:** Original GRI analysis of a third author-supplied partial spoken passage. The conversational term **“NHI” (non-human intellect)** is used by the participants in the supplied passage; it is not adopted here as an official Federal Reserve, IMF or GRI scientific classification. Exact wording and attribution remain subject to checking against an official recording.

Georgieva proposes a three-part policy question: near-term inflation and possible later disinflation, financial stability risk, and productivity upside. She asks which dimension should dominate the central bank's task forces. Warsh declines to privilege a single outcome. His stated approach, as represented in the supplied passage, is to let independent task forces on productivity and jobs, data collection, and inflation generate new intellectual capital for FOMC deliberation rather than merely validate the chair's assumptions. He emphasizes private capital experimentation, the central bank's price-stability and employment remit, financial stability, scenario analysis, negative tail risks, and humility about whether intervention can improve outcomes.

### GRI analytical findings and hypotheses
1. **Triangular policy tradeoff:** AI investment may raise short-run demand and price pressures, enable future productivity-led disinflation, and create financial stability exposure through leverage, valuation reversals, concentrated suppliers or infrastructure bottlenecks. These are conditional mechanisms requiring measurement.
2. **Governance by scenario, not winner-picking:** Regulators need not identify winning firms to assess systemic exposure. Stress scenarios can consider failed AI investment expectations, correlated credit losses, electricity bottlenecks, market concentration, cyber outages and cross-border capital spillovers.
3. **Independent challenge and epistemic resilience:** Task forces are most useful when they can challenge institutional consensus, document assumptions, surface alternative hypotheses, and make their evidentiary limits visible.
4. **Global divide transmission:** A capital reversal or energy shock may affect countries differently according to debt exposure, infrastructure resilience, currency vulnerability and reliance on imported compute. Link these scenarios to World Bank energy-finance history, IMF macroeconomic indicators and Signé's published AI divide framework.
5. **Nelson model:** GRI independently interprets liberalism through private capital allocation and international coordination, economic nationalism through domestic strategic capacity and public risk management, and structuralism through asymmetric exposure and concentration of investment returns.

### Suggested assurance protocol
Record for each scenario: shock definition, transmission mechanism, horizon, affected population, leading indicators, counterevidence, institution with authority to act, feasible mitigation, residual risk, and human reviewer. Distinguish possibility, model estimate, and verified observed event. Do not describe speculative tail risks as actual financial instability.
